90 days without utilities

A major Wasatch Front earthquake could leave hundreds of thousands of Utah homes without safe tap water for three months. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab) Planning for months is different from planning for days: some things you can store, some you can't, and sometimes the plan is to leave.

Make my plan

Federal kit advice is written for several days on your own. Utah's own earthquake modeling runs much longer: in a magnitude 7.0 scenario on the Salt Lake City segment of the Wasatch fault, 332,800 households still have no safe tap water on day 90. This guide scales the basics up to months, and it's honest about what a household can and can't do. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)

Why plan for months

  • In the FEMA-funded scenario for a magnitude 7.0 quake on the Salt Lake City segment, 483,600 households lose safe tap water on day 1, and 332,800 still have none on day 90. These numbers come from a computer model: they estimate one possible outcome, not a prediction. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Power comes back much faster in the same model: 444,600 households lose electricity on day 1, 27,300 are still out on day 30, and 800 on day 90. Water, not power, is what stays out longest. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • A break in one of the large aqueducts (big water pipelines) that serve the Wasatch Front would probably take six months or longer to repair, because the parts have to be custom made. Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab)
  • Having gas doesn't mean having heat: a gas furnace won't run without electricity for its thermostat and fan. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Temporary housing probably won't be available right away, so families may have to fend for themselves longer than they expect, and a Utah winter makes that harder. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Long outages happen. A Harvard-led survey after Hurricane Maria in 2017 found that Puerto Rico households went an average of 84 days without electricity and 68 days without water, counting only through the end of that year. The study tied about one-third of the deaths it estimated to delayed or interrupted medical care. Harvard FXB (opens source in a new tab) NEJM 2018 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Federal agencies also watch threats to the grid itself. NOAA says an extreme geomagnetic storm (space weather caused by activity on the sun) may make some grid systems collapse or black out and may damage transformers. In 2024, the Department of Energy (DOE) reported that the wait for a new large power transformer was commonly quoted at 36 months from ordering. Most transmission operators keep some spare transformers, and DOE estimates that a spare can be moved and switched on in about three to four weeks when the work is rushed. NOAA SWPC (opens source in a new tab) DOE (opens source in a new tab)
  • In October 2026, Fox News reported that the Pentagon's FORTRESS initiative would require every major military base in the continental U.S. to keep running without outside support for at least 90 days. As of Oct. 26, Snopes reported that the Pentagon hadn't confirmed the 90-day figure on the record. The reported plan is for military bases only; it isn't guidance for households. Fox News (opens source in a new tab) Snopes (opens source in a new tab)

Stay or go: decide before you need to

  • The Red Cross says to leave home during an outage if it gets too hot or too cold, or if someone relies on medical devices that need power. Red Cross (opens source in a new tab)
  • Staying in a cold house isn't automatically the safe choice. In Texas's 2021 winter storm, hypothermia (a dangerously low body temperature) caused 158 of 246 deaths. Texas health officials listed refusing to go to heated shelters or warming centers as a risk factor for hypothermia. Texas DSHS (opens source in a new tab) NIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah winters are cold: Salt Lake City's average January low is 24°F, and in Randolph (Rich County) January averages 14 nights at 0°F or colder. NWS (opens source in a new tab) NOAA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Pick several places you could go, in different directions: family or friends outside the area, or a motel. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • In Utah's magnitude 7.0 scenario, damaged homes would need a safety inspection before families could move back in, and inspectors may need many days or weeks. Homes tagged yellow or red would have limits on use, so have somewhere else in mind. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • FEMA's hotel program (Transitional Sheltering Assistance) is offered only after the President declares a disaster and the state asks for it. When it's offered, it can cover households that had to leave because the disaster cut off their utilities, even if the home isn't damaged. FEMA (opens source in a new tab) FEMA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Insurance usually won't pay for leaving just because the power is out: the Utah Insurance Department says purely voluntary evacuations are out-of-pocket. Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab)
  • Many shelters and hotels don't allow pets, so line up a pet-friendly place or a pet buddy now. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)

Staying warm in winter

  • Choose one warm room for everyone, ideally on the highest floor, with a low ceiling and south-facing windows. Block drafts with blankets or towels, but leave some fresh air. Inside that room, make a smaller, warmer sleeping space (a "nest") where everyone sleeps together. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Heat only with equipment made for indoor use. Never use an outdoor or patio heater, grill, camp stove, or charcoal inside your home or in a garage. Never run a generator inside your home, garage, basement, crawlspace, or shed, or on a porch or in a carport: opening doors and windows won't let in enough fresh air to stop deadly carbon monoxide. CPSC (opens source in a new tab) CPSC (opens source in a new tab) CDC (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Burning any fuel makes carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas you can't see or smell. Never heat your home with a gas oven or stovetop. CDC (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Wood stoves and fireplaces: have the chimney inspected every year. After an earthquake, have a professional inspect the fireplace or wood stove and its chimney before you light a fire, because hidden damage can cause a chimney fire or let poisonous gas into the house. Burn only dry, seasoned firewood (wood that was split and left to dry for at least six months), and never use gasoline, kerosene, or lighter fluid to start or relight a fire. Keep a sturdy screen in front of a fireplace. Open the fireplace damper (the flap in the chimney that lets smoke out) before you light a fire, and keep it open until the ashes are cool. Put cool ashes in a metal container kept away from the house. Never burn charcoal in a fireplace. CDC (opens source in a new tab) Cal Poly Humboldt (opens source in a new tab) EPA (opens source in a new tab) NFPA (opens source in a new tab) EPA (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's Division of Air Quality calls mandatory no-burn days when air pollution reaches unhealthy levels, usually in winter. They apply in Salt Lake and Davis counties, Cache Valley, and parts of Box Elder, Tooele, Utah, and Weber counties. On those days you can't use a wood-burning fireplace or a wood, pellet, or coal stove unless it's registered with the Division as your home's only source of heat. The rule doesn't cover gas fireplaces or other natural gas appliances. Before you light a fire, check the day's burn status on the UtahAir app, the Division's website, or local news, and have another way to stay warm. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • Indoor-rated propane heaters still burn fuel. Use one only with at least as much fresh air as its instructions call for, and turn it off before anyone goes to sleep. Its built-in oxygen sensor shuts the heater off if oxygen runs low, but it isn't a carbon monoxide alarm. Never bring a large propane tank indoors. EPA (opens source in a new tab) CPSC (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Kerosene heaters: burn only clear 1-K kerosene, never gasoline, and refuel outdoors only after the heater is off and cool. Run it with the room's door open to the rest of the house. If you must close that door, or your home is newer and tightly built or has been sealed against drafts, also open an outside window about an inch. If it flares up, don't move it, cover it, or put water on it. If it has a shut-off switch, use it, and if the fire doesn't go out, get everyone out right away and call 911. CPSC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Put battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms on every level of your home and near every sleeping area, including your warm room. Check their batteries and keep spares. CDC (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from any heater, and turn portable heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed. Make sure your smoke alarms have battery backup, and test them at least once a month. Keep a fire extinguisher near any room with a major heat source. NFPA (opens source in a new tab) Red Cross (opens source in a new tab) Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab)
  • Know the signs of hypothermia. In adults: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, slurred speech, and drowsiness. In babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. A body temperature below 95°F needs medical care right away. If you can't get help right away, move the person somewhere warm, take off wet clothing, and warm the center of the body (chest, neck, head, and groin) with skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry blankets. Warm drinks can help, but no alcohol, and never give a drink to someone who's unconscious. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Older adults are at risk even in a cool house: the National Institute on Aging says indoor temperatures of 60 to 65°F can cause hypothermia in older adults. NIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Never warm up in a running car inside a garage, even with the garage door open, and keep a running car's tailpipe clear of snow. A small leak in a car's exhaust system can let carbon monoxide build up inside the car, and carbon monoxide can kill people who are asleep before they have any symptoms. CDC (opens source in a new tab) NFPA (opens source in a new tab)

Staying cool in summer

  • Fans help only when it's below 90°F indoors; above that, a fan can raise your body temperature, so go somewhere cooler. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Use a cool room on the lowest floor with north-facing windows. Cover south- and west-facing windows with foil, keep doors and windows closed during the day, and open upstairs windows at night. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • In Utah's dry air, wet sheets hung over windows and doors can cool a room. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Check on older adults at least twice a day in hot weather. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Heat stroke is an emergency: watch for confusion, slurred speech, passing out, seizures, or a body temperature above 103°F. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler, cool them with cool cloths or water while you wait, and don't give them anything to drink. NWS (opens source in a new tab) NIOSH (opens source in a new tab)

Power: what backup can and can't do

  • Rooftop solar alone won't keep your lights on: grid-connected systems shut off when the grid goes down, unless they have a battery and equipment designed for backup. DOE (opens source in a new tab) Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab)
  • Without solar to recharge them, batteries run down fast. A 2021 study for Rocky Mountain Power gave this example: a battery with 10 kilowatt-hours (kWh, a measure of electricity) of usable capacity, charged to 80%, could run a home that uses 24 kWh a day for about 8 hours. Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab)
  • Winter is the hard part. In a Rocky Mountain Power study of all-electric apartments in Herriman, the solar panels made less than half the electricity the apartments used each day in winter, so the batteries were unlikely to recharge during a multi-day outage. Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab)
  • Plan to power a few essentials, not the whole house. A federal lab study found solar plus a 10 kWh battery could cover a short list of essentials (such as a refrigerator, some lights, and phone charging, but not heating or cooling) through a 3-day outage in almost every U.S. county. Berkeley Lab (opens source in a new tab)
  • To work out how big a battery or portable power station (a battery pack with built-in outlets) you need, start with your refrigerator: the Department of Energy says an average refrigerator-freezer uses about 588 kWh a year, about 1.6 kWh a day by our arithmetic. DOE (opens source in a new tab)
  • Be Ready Utah says generators are only for short outages, because they need a lot of fuel and can break down. Use one to buy time while you switch to plans that don't need power. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • A portable generator burns roughly half to three-quarters of a gallon of fuel an hour, about 12 to 18 gallons a day. By our arithmetic, 90 days would take more than a thousand gallons, over 100 times the 10 gallons the fire code allows in a private garage without a storage cabinet. UF Extension (opens source in a new tab) 2024 IFC (Utah) (opens source in a new tab)
  • Run a generator only outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, never on a porch or in a carport, with its exhaust pointed away from windows, doors, and vents. Keep it dry: touching a wet generator, or anything plugged into it, can shock you. On average, carbon monoxide from portable generators kills nearly 100 people a year in the U.S. CPSC (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or connect it to your home's breaker or fuse box: that sends power back into the utility's lines (called backfeed), a dangerous hazard for line crews. To power your home's wiring, have a licensed electrician install a transfer switch, which cuts your home off from the utility's lines. Otherwise, plug appliances straight into the generator with heavy-duty, grounded (three-prong) extension cords rated for the load. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Let a generator cool before refueling; Be Ready Utah says to wait 20 minutes. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Small power banks and solar chargers are a low-cost way to keep phones charged. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab)
  • If someone at home depends on powered medical equipment, tell your power company. Rocky Mountain Power customers can call 1-888-221-7070 and ask about its Medical Certificate program, which doesn't guarantee your power stays on. If another utility serves you, ask what it offers. Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab) Rocky Mountain Power (opens source in a new tab)

Fuel: store less than you think, and store it safely

  • Utah's fire code has used the 2024 International Fire Code since July 1, 2026. It allows up to 10 gallons of gasoline or similar fuel for equipment, in approved containers, in a private garage; more needs a storage cabinet. That's the legal limit, not the best place: Be Ready Utah says not to store fuel in your home or an attached garage, and an outbuilding like a shed is best. Never store fuel in a basement. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) 2024 IFC (Utah) (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Lehi Fire (opens source in a new tab)
  • Lehi's fire department recommends keeping no more than 5 gallons of gasoline at home, and no more than 25 gallons of propane in portable cylinders, which is five 20-pound grill tanks. Ask your own fire department about local limits. Lehi Fire (opens source in a new tab)
  • In federal lab tests, sealed gasoline with 10% ethanol still met fuel-quality standards after a year, though fuel stored other ways may age differently. If a generator will sit for more than a few months, Be Ready Utah says to use up the fuel in its tank or add fuel stabilizer. Use stored fuel over time and replace it with fresh fuel. ORNL (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Lehi Fire (opens source in a new tab)
  • Store propane cylinders upright and outdoors, away from heat and direct sunlight. Never keep them in a basement, where heavier-than-air propane can collect. Utah requires stored cylinders to sit at least 10 feet from doorways and other building openings. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) 2024 IFC (opens source in a new tab) Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • A 20-pound propane tank gives about 18 to 20 hours of cooking. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't refill disposable 1-pound propane bottles. Bottles marked "DOT-39" are made to be used only once, and federal law forbids transporting one that's been refilled. 49 CFR 178.65 (opens source in a new tab)

Water for 90 days

  • At 1 gallon per person per day, 90 days is 90 gallons per person, and that's a minimum. Utah's Division of Emergency Management says children, seniors, nursing mothers, people on medication or with health conditions, and pets need extra, and Utah's dry climate may call for more. For a family of four that's at least 360 gallons, about 3,000 pounds by our arithmetic, so store what you can and keep supplies to treat more. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) JVWCD (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's Division of Emergency Management says start with a 14-day supply and work up to 30 days. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Your home's pipes may hold 20 to 40 gallons of clean water, and a water heater about 40 more. After an earthquake, shut your main water valve so this water stays clean even if the public supply isn't, and assume a boil order is in effect until officials say otherwise. Before using the water heater's water, turn off its gas or power as its manual says and let the water cool, then drain it from the spigot at the bottom. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) JVWCD (opens source in a new tab)
  • If water is cloudy, let it settle, then filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter. Boil it at a rolling boil for at least 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 5,000 feet. If you can't boil, use regular unscented bleach less than a year old: 8 drops of 6% (or 6 drops of 8.25%) per gallon, doubled for cloudy, colored, or very cold water. Stir and wait 30 minutes; the water should smell slightly of chlorine, and if it doesn't, add the same dose again and wait 15 more minutes. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • To remove parasites, a portable filter needs an absolute pore size (the size of its holes) of 1 micron or smaller. Filters don't remove viruses, so disinfect filtered water too. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Boiling or disinfecting can't make water safe if it contains fuel, toxic chemicals, or radioactive material. Use another source, and ask your health department for advice. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't drink pool, hot tub, or waterbed water even after treatment, and use toilet-tank water only for flushing and cleaning. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah law lets you collect rainwater without registering in up to two covered containers of 100 gallons or less each, or up to 2,500 gallons with free registration, for use on the same property. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) Utah Water Rights (opens source in a new tab) Like any water of unknown safety, treat it before drinking. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Half an inch of rain on a 20-by-40-foot roof yields about 175 gallons. USU Extension (opens source in a new tab)
  • After a disaster, listen for instructions from officials, who may tell you where to get water and other supplies. Keep clean containers you can carry, because some distribution sites fill only the containers people bring. Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab) EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Outside help takes time: Salt Lake City Emergency Management estimates that after a magnitude 7.0 quake on the Salt Lake City segment, it will be at least 96 hours before resources from outside the area begin to arrive. SLC Emergency Management (opens source in a new tab)
  • A private well stops when its pump loses power; have a licensed electrician set up any generator hookup. Virginia Dept. of Health (opens source in a new tab)

Sanitation when sewers fail

  • After an earthquake, don't flush or pour anything down drains until you know the sewer lines are safe. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Use a two-bucket toilet. The first bucket has no bag and is only for urine. The second is only for solid waste: line it with two heavy-duty plastic bags, put a one-inch layer of kitty litter in the bottom, and cover the waste with about two cups of litter after each use. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Wear disposable gloves, eye protection, and a mask whenever you handle full bags of waste. Never dump waste in streams, canals, or anywhere water may flow, and never leave it on the ground. Bury waste only if you have a safe spot at least 200 feet from any water supply or living area, and never in a garden. Dig the hole 2.5 to 3 feet deep. Open each bag, empty it into the hole, put the empty bag in too, and cover it with dirt. Between uses, cover the hole with boards and heavy objects to keep animals and children out. When the waste is about a foot from the top, stop using that hole and fill it with dirt. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you can't bury waste safely, or the ground is frozen, store the closed bags for a short time instead. Use a garbage can of 30 gallons or less with a tight lid, and strap the lid down with rope or bungee cords. Keep the can outside or in a garage, fastened to a strong post, tree, or other sturdy structure so it can't be knocked over. Ask your local health department how to get rid of the stored bags. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep at least three months of toilet paper, menstrual products, and diapers. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Wash hands with soap and clean water for at least 20 seconds, and set up a handwashing station with a large jug of water. Use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol only when you can't wash. CDC (opens source in a new tab) CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • If your septic system has a pump, the pump stops when the power goes out. Virginia's health department says most such systems should have room for only about 100 to 200 gallons above the high-water alarm, and going past that can spill raw sewage, so use as little water as you can. Virginia Dept. of Health (opens source in a new tab)

Food and cooking

  • Eat perishable food first, like meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and leftovers. Keep the doors closed: a closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours, and a full freezer about 48 hours (24 if half full). Throw out perishable food that's been above 40°F for 2 hours or more. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Red Cross (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't use snow or the porch as a refrigerator: temperatures swing, and snow can carry dirt and germs. Illinois Extension (opens source in a new tab)
  • Build a three-month supply of foods you normally eat, then add long-term staples like wheat, white rice, and beans. The Food storage guide covers how. Church home storage (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Plan for variety and vitamin C. USU Extension says scurvy, an illness caused by too little vitamin C, is the most likely nutrition problem on stored food, and a BYU food-storage researcher says vitamin C tablets keep a high share of their strength for more than 20 years. USU Extension (opens source in a new tab) BYU (opens source in a new tab)
  • Use grills and camp stoves only outdoors, never in a garage, and keep camp stoves and charcoal grills at least 20 feet from windows. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) Indoors, Utah's Division of Emergency Management says you can cook on a wood stove or in a fireplace (burn only wood, never charcoal). Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) After an earthquake, have a professional inspect a wood stove or fireplace before you light a fire in it. Cal Poly Humboldt (opens source in a new tab) On mandatory no-burn days, you can't burn a wood stove or fireplace, even to cook, unless it's registered as your home's only source of heat. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) If you smell gas, don't strike a match or use anything that could spark: leave right away and call 911 from a safe distance. If a gas stove may have been damaged, don't light it until your gas company or a qualified professional has checked it. Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) If you don't smell gas and the stove isn't damaged, some gas stoves still work without electricity: check your stove's manual, then light the burners with a match. Cook indoors only in a well-ventilated room, with a window opened slightly if you can. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • A solar oven needs no fuel and still works on clear winter days, just more slowly. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's Division of Emergency Management suggests fuel for two weeks of cooking: about two 20-pound propane tanks or two bags of charcoal. Ninety days would take roughly six times that, by our arithmetic, which is more propane than Lehi's fire department recommends keeping at home, so lean on no-cook foods and a solar oven too. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) Lehi Fire (opens source in a new tab)
  • Cook small batches and open only what you'll eat, since leftovers can't be kept without refrigeration. Illinois Extension (opens source in a new tab)
  • For babies, use ready-to-feed formula if you can. If you must mix formula, use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort. Never make homemade formula. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) FDA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Store plenty of pet food; pets adjust to diet changes poorly. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)

Health and medicine

  • Ask your doctor and your insurance plan about 90-day supplies now. In Utah, a pharmacist may fill a prescription and one or more of its refills at the same time, up to a 100-day supply in all. This doesn't apply to controlled substances; ask your pharmacist whether any of your medicines are controlled. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's emergency refill rule is narrow. If your prescriber can't be reached quickly, a pharmacist may give one emergency refill of a medicine you already take, but only as much as the emergency requires. If that pharmacy has the prescription on file, it can give more than a 3-day supply only if the prescription has no refills left or expired in the past 30 days. Even then, it can't give more than a 30-day supply or your last fill there, whichever is less. The rule doesn't cover controlled substances; ask your pharmacist whether any of your medicines are controlled. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • Insulin: if your insulin prescription has no refills left or expired within the past six months, a Utah pharmacist can give a one-time emergency supply of up to 60 days. Insulin in its original vial or cartridge can stay out of the refrigerator for up to 28 days at 59 to 86°F. Keep it as cool as you can without letting it freeze, and away from direct heat and sunlight; don't use insulin that froze. If it got hotter than 86°F and you can't replace it, you may still need to use it until you can. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) FDA (opens source in a new tab)
  • If the power is out for more than a day, medicine that needs refrigeration should generally be replaced unless its label says otherwise; ask a pharmacist or doctor. But don't throw away a medicine you need to stay alive, like insulin, if you can't replace it quickly: FDA says it may be used until you get a new supply. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) FDA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Powered medical equipment: ask the company that supplies it whether it can run on batteries or a generator, and about a backup that doesn't need electricity, such as oxygen cylinders. Keep spare batteries and a car charger, and plan where you'd go. Never plug an oxygen concentrator into an extension cord. Don't lower your oxygen flow to make your batteries or oxygen last longer unless your doctor says it's OK. American Lung Association (opens source in a new tab) FDA (opens source in a new tab) American Lung Association (opens source in a new tab) Oxygen makes things burn more easily. Keep heaters, stoves, fireplaces, and any other heat or flame at least five feet from where oxygen is used or stored, and don't cook on a gas stove while you're using oxygen. American Lung Association (opens source in a new tab)
  • Dialysis centers may close without power or water. Learn the 3-Day Emergency Diet, a strict diet for when you miss treatments; it doesn't replace dialysis, so always try to get dialysis within three days of your last treatment. ESRD Network 15 (Comagine Health), the End Stage Renal Disease network for Utah and five nearby states, can tell you where to find dialysis facilities: 1-800-262-1514. CMS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't make the hospital your only backup plan: Utah modeling of a magnitude 7 Salt Lake quake estimated only about 38% of hospital beds would be available on the day of the quake. In an emergency, still call 911. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Months of disaster stress are real. The Disaster Distress Helpline is toll-free and open 24/7: call or text 1-800-985-5990 (standard text message rates apply). Calls to 988 from Utah reach the Utah Crisis Line. SAMHSA (opens source in a new tab) Huntsman Mental Health (opens source in a new tab)

Light, news, and staying in touch

  • Use flashlights and battery lanterns, not candles: candles start about 5,900 U.S. home fires a year (2020 to 2024 average), and sleep was a factor in about a third of candle-fire deaths. NFPA (opens source in a new tab) USFA (opens source in a new tab) If anyone at home uses medical oxygen, use only battery-powered lights, never candles or gas lights. FDA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't count on cell service: after Hurricane Helene, lost power was the leading reason cell towers and other cell sites in western North Carolina went out of service. FCC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Text instead of calling; texts get through more reliably on overloaded networks. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep a battery or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio, which picks up the National Weather Service's own broadcasts. Utah's NOAA Weather Radio stations broadcast weather warnings around the clock, and emergency officials also use them to share information about other hazards, including after an earthquake. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) NWS (opens source in a new tab) NWS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Two-way radios: Family Radio Service (FRS) radios need no license. General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS) radios need an FCC license, which costs $35 and covers your immediate family. Amateur (ham) radio requires passing an exam. 47 CFR 95.305 (opens source in a new tab) 47 CFR 95.1705 (opens source in a new tab) 47 CFR 1.1102 (opens source in a new tab) 47 CFR 97.501 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Charging phones from a car? Run it only fully outdoors, never in a garage, and keep the tailpipe clear of snow. CDC (opens source in a new tab) NFPA (opens source in a new tab)

Money and daily life

  • A long outage can close grocery stores, gas stations, ATMs, and banks, so keep some cash in small bills. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Gas stations need electricity to pump: after Hurricane Irma, about 40% of Florida stations were still closed days later, largely because of power outages. NPR (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep copies of important documents in more than one place, including a secure cloud copy. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you lose work, including self-employment, because of a major disaster the President has declared, and you don't qualify for regular unemployment, Disaster Unemployment Assistance may cover weeks out of work up to 26 weeks after the declaration. Apply through your state unemployment office. U.S. DOL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Never pay anyone to help you get FEMA aid: FEMA and state workers never ask for money. Check a FEMA ID by calling 1-800-621-3362. CFPB (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's price-gouging law, which limits price hikes during emergencies, applies only in the affected area: while a governor's state of emergency lasts, or for the first 30 days after a presidential declaration. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)

Neighbors and community

  • After a major earthquake, expect fewer responders than usual: Utah's FEMA-funded scenario for a magnitude 7.0 earthquake on the Wasatch fault at Salt Lake City estimates that 30% of first responders, officials, and support staff won't be able to do their jobs. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Decades of disaster research find that looting and violent crime are rare in the emergency period of disasters; most people help each other. Univ. of Delaware DRC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's free Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) course takes 24 to 30 hours and teaches fire safety, light search and rescue, and disaster first aid. Pair it with the Neighborhood plan guide. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints keeps an emergency response plan in each ward (local congregation) and stake (group of congregations). Church emergency preparedness (opens source in a new tab)

Protecting the house

  • If you leave in winter and can't keep the heat on, turn off the water heater first (its power if it's electric, or the gas valve on the heater itself if it's gas). Then shut off the main water valve. Purdue Extension (opens source in a new tab) Kentucky Division of Water (opens source in a new tab) If your home has fire sprinklers, ask your insurer before you shut off the water: a standard policy says you must take reasonable care to keep both the water and the heat on. ISO HO-3 sample (opens source in a new tab)
  • Next, drain the pipes. Normally you open every faucet, hot and cold, until the water stops. Kentucky Division of Water (opens source in a new tab) But after an earthquake, don't let water go down the drains or flush the toilets until you know the sewer lines are safe. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) And if you have a septic system with a pump and the power is out, Virginia's health department says most such systems should have room for only about 100 to 200 gallons above the high-water alarm. Virginia Dept. of Health (opens source in a new tab) In either case, open the highest faucet in the house to let air in, and catch the water in clean containers at the lowest faucet. If you shut the main valve soon after the quake, this water can be clean drinking water to take with you; if you aren't sure, treat it before you drink it. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) JVWCD (opens source in a new tab)
  • Empty the toilet tanks and bowls; after an earthquake, do it without flushing. Don't drink toilet-tank water. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Empty the drain traps (the bend in the pipe under each sink, tub, and floor drain) or fill them with RV antifreeze (propylene glycol). The water in the water heater may still be very hot, so let it cool in the tank first. Then drain the water heater and other water-using appliances, but don't put antifreeze in them, and never put car antifreeze anywhere in a home water system. Purdue Extension (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Insulation alone won't protect pipes through a long freeze. If a pipe freezes, a plumber may be your best call. To thaw it yourself, shut off the main water valve, open the faucet on that line, and warm the pipe slowly, for example with towels soaked in hot (not boiling) water. Never use a torch or other open flame; it can start a fire. USU Extension (opens source in a new tab)
  • Insurance: a standard homeowners policy covers freezing damage only if you took reasonable care to keep the heat on, or to shut off the water and drain all pipes and appliances. If your home has fire sprinklers, you must take reasonable care to keep both the water and the heat on. Vandalism and broken-glass coverage can stop once a home has been vacant more than 60 days in a row. Ask your insurer before you leave. ISO HO-3 sample (opens source in a new tab) NAIC (opens source in a new tab) Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't shut off natural gas just because you're leaving. If you smell gas or hear it hissing, get everyone out, don't use light switches or anything else that could spark, and call 911 from a safe distance. Turn the gas off at the meter only if it's safe and you suspect a leak, see structural damage to the building, see a fire near the meter or gas pipes, or officials or Enbridge Gas tell you to. Enbridge Gas delivers natural gas to customers in Utah, Wyoming, and Idaho. Once the gas is off, only Enbridge or a qualified professional can turn it back on. Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) Red Cross (opens source in a new tab)
  • Tell your out-of-state contact where you're going, and leave a note saying when you left. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Before you go, unplug electronics and small appliances, including space heaters, to avoid damage from power surges when the power comes back. Leave the refrigerator and freezer plugged in unless flooding is a risk. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Red Cross (opens source in a new tab) If you can keep the heat on, leave it set no lower than 55°F. Red Cross (opens source in a new tab)
  • Coming home: if you smell gas, don't go in; move away and call 911 from a safe distance. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) Then check the vents outside the house for your furnace and water heater (the pipes their exhaust comes out of) and clear away any snow and ice. Enbridge Gas says to keep vents clear, and in Texas's 2021 winter storm, ice blocking the vents of gas heating equipment caused some of the deaths from carbon monoxide. Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) Texas DSHS (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you drained the water, close the drain spigot at the bottom of the water heater and put back any drain plugs or traps you took off. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Purdue Extension (opens source in a new tab) After an earthquake, wait until you know the sewer lines are safe, because refilling the pipes sends water down the drains. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Then turn the main valve back on and run each faucet, hot and cold, until the pipes are full again. Kentucky Division of Water (opens source in a new tab) Make sure the water heater is full before you turn its power back on, or before a professional turns its gas back on. FEMA (opens source in a new tab) After an earthquake, assume a boil order is in effect until officials say otherwise. JVWCD (opens source in a new tab)
  • If the gas was turned off, at the meter or at the water heater's own valve, don't turn it back on or relight anything yourself: Enbridge Gas or a qualified professional should. Don't relight a gas appliance that may be damaged, either. Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) FEMA (opens source in a new tab) Throw out perishable food that's been above 40°F for 2 hours or more, or that has an unusual smell, color, or texture. If you can't tell how warm it got or for how long, throw it out. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
