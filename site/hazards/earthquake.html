Earthquake

Utah is earthquake country. The good news: the most useful steps are cheap, and you can do most of them this weekend.

Add this to my plan

Most Utahns live near the Wasatch Fault. A large earthquake would come with no warning, so preparation happens beforehand: secure heavy things, know how to Drop, Cover, and Hold On, and be ready to manage without water, power, or sewer for a while.

Why it matters in Utah

  • About 85% of Utah's residents, infrastructure, and economy sit within 15 miles of the Wasatch fault, the state's most active fault. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Scientists estimate more than a 50% chance of a magnitude 6.5 or larger earthquake along the Wasatch Front within 50 years, and say the Wasatch fault can produce an earthquake as large as about M7.6. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah has had 17 earthquakes larger than M5.5 since pioneer settlement in 1847. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • On March 18, 2020, the M5.7 Magna earthquake shook the Salt Lake Valley. It is the most recent strong reminder. USGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah has no public earthquake early warning system. ShakeAlert operates in California, Oregon, and Washington; Utah has studied feasibility but does not have alerts. Never count on a warning. UGS (opens source in a new tab) ShakeAlert (USGS & partners) (opens source in a new tab)
Show the science
  • Liquefaction. Loose, water-saturated sandy soil can behave like a liquid during strong shaking. UGS says Wasatch Front valleys are especially vulnerable because of susceptible soils and shallow groundwater; the most susceptible soils are generally along rivers, streams, and lake shorelines. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Basin shaking. After Magna, researchers analyzing thousands of recordings found that sites on deep Salt Lake basin sediments shook roughly 3 to 10 times more than thin-sediment sites at longer periods, which matters most for taller buildings. That range compares groups of sites; it is not a forecast for any one address. Moschetti et al. 2021 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Fault geometry. Research on Magna suggests the Wasatch fault may flatten with depth, which could mean stronger shaking in Salt Lake City than earlier estimates. This is an active research result, not settled consensus. Pang et al. 2020 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Scenario modeling. UGS cites scenario modeling for a major M7.0 earthquake on the Salt Lake City segment of 2,000 to 2,500 deaths, 55,400 buildings completely damaged, and $33.2 billion in short-term direct losses. Scenarios are planning tools, not predictions. UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • The national expert hazard model (USGS 2023) now includes basin-specific data for Salt Lake City. USGS (opens source in a new tab)

Who's most affected

  • People in unreinforced masonry (URM) brick buildings. URM buildings are more likely than modern structures to collapse during an earthquake; Utah building codes outlawed new URM construction in the 1970s. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Households on valley floors near rivers, streams, and old lakeshores (liquefaction). UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Anyone who depends on power, pharmacies, or home care, because services may be out for days or longer.

What to do

1 Before

  • Practice Drop, Cover, and Hold On. Utahns drill together in the Great Utah ShakeOut, and you can register a household drill for any day of the year. Utah ShakeOut (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Strap your water heater, which also protects stored water. Be Ready Utah lists water-heater straps as part of water prep. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Secure tall furniture, TVs, and heavy wall hangings, and move heavy things off high shelves, especially above beds. Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep sturdy shoes, a flashlight, and work gloves by each bed. Broken glass is common after shaking. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Learn where your gas, water, and electric shutoffs are, and when to use them. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Store water and food for at least two weeks. Water and sewer lines can break. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Look up your address on Know Your Ground, from our sister site FaultLine Utah, for nearby faults, liquefaction, and building-code shaking in plain language. The UGS Geologic Hazards Portal has the full maps. FaultLine Utah (opens source in a new tab) UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • If your home is older brick, learn the URM warning signs (below), walk through the Is It Brick? self-check, and talk to a licensed structural engineer about retrofit options. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) Is It Brick? (opens source in a new tab)

2 During

  • Drop to your hands and knees, Cover your head and neck under a sturdy table or desk (or against an interior wall), and Hold On until shaking stops. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're in bed, turn face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're inside, stay inside; don't run outside, and avoid doorways. URM buildings can crumble onto sidewalks around them. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're in a car, pull over, stop, and set the parking brake. If you're outside, move to an open area away from buildings, trees, streetlights, and power lines. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)

3 After

  • Expect aftershocks. Drop, Cover, and Hold On each time. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Check people for injuries, then look for damage, leaking gas and water lines, and downed power lines. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're in a damaged building, go outside and move away from it, and don't go back into damaged buildings. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're trapped, send a text or bang on a pipe or wall, and use a whistle instead of shouting. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Use your stored water. Don't drink from pipes until officials say the water is safe. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Report what you felt to USGS "Did You Feel It?" through the event page; it helps scientists. USGS (opens source in a new tab)

Is my building unreinforced masonry (URM)?

Utah DEM lists exterior signs that a building might be URM. None of them alone proves it, so treat them as reasons to ask a professional: Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)

  • Rows of bricks turned endways (you see the short end) every five to six rows.
  • Deeply recessed windows set into thick walls.
  • Rows of bolt heads or rosettes on the exterior walls.

Salt Lake City's Fix the Bricks program helps low-income SLC owners of brick homes built before 1975, but it is closed to new applicants with a stated wait of more than 10 years; its page lists an average retrofit cost of $20,000. Salt Lake City (opens source in a new tab) Renters: you can still ask your landlord whether the building is URM and whether it has been retrofitted (see your personalized plan).
